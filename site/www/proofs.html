Proving that no better packing exists

Finding a good packing is a search. Proving that nothing smaller can work is a different job: you have to rule out every arrangement at once, including ones nobody has thought of. Almost every proof in this subject uses one trick, due to Frits Göbel (1979): unavoidable points.

The trick

Suppose you want to show that n unit squares can't fit in a square box of side s. Scatter some points in the box so that every possible position of a unit square — anywhere, at any angle — covers at least one of them. If you managed it with fewer than n points, you're done: n squares with no overlap would each need their own point, and there aren't enough to go round.

The whole art is choosing the points. Near a wall a unit square can't tilt much without poking out, so a row of points about 0.9 from each wall catches everything there. In the middle, a triangle of points with sides ≤ 1 can't be dodged (a unit square whose centre is in the triangle must cover a corner of it). Stitching such lemmas together over the whole box is the proof.

A sharper version asks each point to be hit not once but k times: if every unit square covers at least k of m points, then at most ⌊m/k⌋ squares fit. Below is a real one.

Try it: 81 points, every square catches 7

In a box of side 35/9 ≈ 3.889 there are 81 points such that every unit square inside the box, at every position and angle, contains at least 7 of them. Twelve squares would need 12 × 7 = 84. So twelve unit squares fit in no box smaller than this: s(12) ≥ 35/9.

Drag the square around and rotate it. The points it covers light up and the count is shown. Try to get it below 7 — you can't, but the places where it is exactly 7 tell you what the points are for.

The map samples 120 × 120 positions; the real proof checks the whole continuum of positions and angles with exact integer arithmetic (an open-source verifier), and a proof assistant checks the counting step. Points exactly on the square's edge count as covered. That convention is what lets a packing in any smaller box be scaled up into this one and refuted, which is why the conclusion covers every box smaller than 35/9. The skeleton is the same in every such set found: a # of lines one unit from each wall, and a small ring at the centre.

Why twelve is still open

Everyone believes s(12) = 4: the plain 4 × 4 grid with four squares missing. The best proven floor is now 3.9686 — our own result, computer-checked but not refereed — from a weighted version of the same argument where points carry fractional weights chosen by a linear program, an idea from Sam Burns's and Gustavo Massaccesi's 2026 work on n = 17 (Sources §5). Weighted points alone cannot finish the job: at box side 4 itself no weighting of points totals less than 12.27 (proved exactly), so closing the last 0.03 needs case analysis on top — the kind used to prove s(13) = 4, which is next.

Walkthrough: how Bentz ruled out thirteen

Bentz works in the square [0,4]² with boxes — the inside of a square of side a little more than 1. If 13 boxes never fit in [0,4]², then 13 unit squares need a container of side at least 4, which is s(13) = 4. He starts with 16 unavoidable points: A(1, 0.914), B(0.914, 1), C(0.914, 2) and D(1.65, 1.65), mirrored in x = 2, y = 2 and y = x — eight points near the corners, four near the middles of the walls, four in the interior. Thirteen boxes against sixteen points leaves only three points of slack, which is enough to force at least two boxes to be alone with a corner point. A box in that position is very restricted: swapping its point for a nearby one leaves the set unavoidable, so the box has to cover that nearby point too (the replacement lemma). From there the argument splits on whether those two lonely boxes sit at the same corner or at different ones, and the split has six leaves.

Click through the leaves and watch the number in the tree: every branch ends at 12. The bookkeeping is "free points + boxes already placed", and a contradiction needs that total to be under 13. Each configuration Bentz builds is, in effect, a certificate worth exactly 12 — so it kills a thirteenth box and nothing more. For 12 squares every leaf would have to come out at 11 instead, and only one of the six does. The loss starts at the very first step: with 12 boxes and 16 points the slack is 4, all eight corner points can pair up in four boxes, and there is no lonely box for the replacement lemma to work on. That single missing unit is why the same proof says nothing about s(12).

Thirteen again, with no cases

The six leaves above are the price of working with plain points. Give the points fractional weights, let a linear program choose them, and the case tree can be made to disappear altogether: the same theorem now follows from a single object, with nothing to click through.

In [0,4]² there are 3,621 points carrying rational weights of total 12.955972, such that every closed unit square inside the box, at every position and every angle, captures weight at least 1 — a point sitting exactly on the square's edge counts. Since 12.955972 < 13, thirteen squares don't fit below side 4: s(13) = 4.

The counting is the same as ever, only with weights. Thirteen unit squares packed into a box smaller than 4 can be blown up until they are squares of side a little more than 1 inside [0,4]², each strictly containing a unit square; those thirteen unit squares are then pairwise disjoint as sets, so no point is credited twice. Each captures weight ≥ 1, which would need a total of 13, and the whole set weighs less. Sixteen unit squares tile [0,4]², so side 4 is reached — the bound is exactly right.

That "closed" convention, boundary points included, is not a detail; it is what makes 13 the number to beat rather than 16. The sixteen squares of the 4 × 4 tiling meet along the grid lines, and a point on a shared edge is credited to both squares that touch it. In the other convention — squares shrunk by a hair, boundary not counted — those sixteen become genuinely disjoint, every cover of [0,4]² would have to weigh at least 16, and nothing below 16 could ever be proved. Closed semantics is also what every published proof in this subject is really using, since they all argue about squares of side 1 + ε for every ε > 0. There is a floor even so: no cover of [0,4]² can weigh less than 12.2688, proved exactly, so the whole proof lives in the narrow room between that and 13 — and, incidentally, no cover of this box will ever say anything about twelve squares, because 12.2688 is already above 12.

Checking such a claim is harder than checking the 81-point set above, because here the margin is zero: at the corner placement the only point the square catches lies exactly on its edge, so there is no slack to sample with. The check has to be exhaustive and exact. Two programs do it, written from the statement rather than from each other and sharing no code — one in Python using exact rational arithmetic, one in Rust using exact integers — and both chop the three-dimensional space of positions and angles into boxes until every box is settled by an exact argument, with none left over. They also pass 23 rejection tests: deliberately broken and mutated certificates that a checker must refuse, including two earlier covers that really were invalid. The lemmas the two checkers rest on are proved in the Lean proof assistant; the subdivision code itself is not, which is worth saying plainly.

The theorem is Bentz's (2010) — only the proof is new, and it is a computation, not something a reader checks by hand. Certificate, both checkers, the rejection tests and the Lean are in the repository (directory s12/); the self-contained write-up is notes/s13-casefree.md, with the exact floor on covers of [0,4]² in search/COVER4.md.
