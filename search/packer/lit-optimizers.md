# Literature: numerical optimizers for packing (aimed at s(n), n ≈ 50–300)

Compiled 2026-10-04 by literature search.  Citations were checked against Crossref/arXiv where marked [verified].  Anything taken from memory and not re-checked is marked **[unverified]**.  No downloaded code was executed.  Ellsworth's tool sources were read as text only, in the scratchpad.

Context: our engine uses a fixed side, a SAT-penetration² + wall energy, L-BFGS, basin hopping and a squeeze.  It reproduces s(11) and s(17) but jams at n ≈ 50–110 into full rows of k axis-aligned squares, and it cannot reproduce Cantrell's s(110) < 11.

---

## 1. Squares-in-square: who found the records, and how

**Ellsworth's live table** (successor to Friedman's page): https://kingbird.myphotos.cc/packing/squares_in_squares.html.  As of Oct 2026 the attributions name the methods:
- "simulated annealing program" (Thomas Schadt, Dec 2025 – Jan 2026: n = 29, 39, 41, 50, 51, 71, …)
- "modified version of Thomas Schadt's simulated annealing program" (Ellsworth, 2025–26; n = 306 in Feb 2026)
- "evolutionary beam search with simulated annealing" (Joost de Winter, 2026, "working with unspecified AI"; n = 68, 126)
- "L-BFGS program" (Jake Loyd, Sep 2026, n = 68)
- LLM-assisted refinements: "Refined by Tej Stead in June 2026, working with Claude Fable 5" (n = 103, **110**, 179); Grigoriy Dyachkov with Claude (n = 152); Allen Chang and Ruilin Wang with GPT-6 Astra (n = 68).
- s(110) ≈ 10.99679 is credited to David W. Cantrell (Feb 2025), refined by Stead (Jun 2026).

I read these through a summarizing fetch, so recheck the exact wording on the page before quoting it.  Schadt's annealer is **not public as far as I found**.  The page gives no algorithm details beyond "annealing".  A 2026 popular essay (H. Gill, Tom Rocks Maths, https://tomrocksmaths.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/square-packing-trm-essay-harry-gill.pdf) calls the "Gensane–Ryckelynck algorithm" and "Schadt's annealing program" the two key programs, the latter "acting as a continuation of the former".  It is secondary and non-technical.

**Ellsworth's tools repo** (public): https://github.com/Davidebyzero/packing_squares_in_squares__tools
- `refine_packing.cpp`: GMP arbitrary-precision post-processing.  It finds minimum-depth SAT separation vectors and runs cascading "nudge" passes that push overlapping pairs apart by 1.01× or 1.0025× the depth, with stall detection.  It also has an "I-strip shift" (move a strip from one edge to the opposite edge) and a step that snaps nearly-orthogonal squares onto the grid anchored at the nearest container corner.  This is polishing, not search.  The corner-anchored grid snap is a direct example of "exactly solved axis-aligned regions".
- `enum-45deg-rect-packings.cpp` (Ellsworth, 2025-01-27 and 2025-12-10): **exhaustive enumeration of a parametrized gadget family**.  It covers rectangular generalizations of Göbel strips/squares: a 45° block, axis-aligned fill in the corners, and corner/edge counts as integer parameters, giving a closed-form s for each n ≤ 2117.  This is exactly the "structured gadget search" idea, already used for records (e.g. n = 2043).
- `check_packing.py`: arbitrary-precision (Decimal) overlap verifier.

**Gensane & Ryckelynck**, "Improved dense packings of congruent squares in a square", *Discrete Comput. Geom.* 34 (2005) 97–109, doi:10.1007/s00454-004-1129-z [verified].  Their related papers are "Dense packings of equal spheres in a cube", *EJC* 11 (2004) R33, doi:10.37236/1786, which uses a "perturbed billiard algorithm", and "Producing dense packings of cubes", *Discrete Math.* 308 (2008) 5230–5245, doi:10.1016/j.disc.2007.09.047 [verified].  The squares algorithm is **[unverified details]** a stochastic hard-constraint method in the same family: random small translations/rotations accepted only if feasible, an inflating scale, and periodic systematic perturbations.  It found the then-best n = 29 and an alternative s(18) (Friedman's survey).  No public code.

**Friedman**, "Packing unit squares in squares: a survey and new results", *EJC* Dynamic Survey DS7 (versions 1998–2009), doi:10.37236/28 [verified].  It is mostly constructions: human-found families (Göbel 45° strips, Stenlund, Cantrell, Hajba, Trump), with a few computer-found entries.  Stenlund (≈1980), Cantrell (2002 onward) and Hajba are credited with hand/geometric constructions **[method details unverified, no published method papers found]**.

**Nagamochi**, "Packing unit squares in a rectangle", *EJC* 12 (2005) R37, doi:10.37236/1934 [verified].  It proves lower bounds for axis-aligned/rectangle cases, not a search method.

**Chung & Graham**, "Efficient packings of unit squares in a large square", *DCG* 64 (2020) 690–699, doi:10.1007/s00454-019-00088-9 [verified], plus a 2025 note by Arslanov & Bui, doi:10.1007/s00454-025-00767-w.  Asymptotic wasted-space constructions: axis-aligned blocks plus slightly tilted "staircase" strips.  These are useful gadget templates for large n.

**AI-driven work.**
- Georgiev, Gómez-Serrano, Tao, Wagner, "Mathematical exploration and discovery at scale" (AlphaEvolve), arXiv:2511.02864 (2025) [verified].  Problem 6.35 ("packing in a dilate") includes n unit squares in a square.  The score was s + Σ area(Pᵢ∩Pⱼ), with the container scale found by binary search.  They report improvements only for hexagons (n = 11, 12) and cubes (n = 11), with **no square records**.  They note the evolved hexagon packings were not even made symmetric, and a human had to refine them.
- Berthold, Kamp, Mexi, Pokutta, Polik, "Out-of-the-box global optimization for packing problems", arXiv:2605.04850 (May 2026) [verified].  They use FICO Xpress/SCIP global solvers with a **Farkas-based separating-hyperplane non-overlap formulation** for regular polygons, and code is at https://github.com/DominikKamp/Packing.  Their improvement table for polygons in a square (ℓ = 4) lists only triangles (m = 3), so there are no squares-in-square records, and these are small-n instances.  The formulation itself (a separating direction αᵢⱼ and offset dᵢⱼ per pair as explicit variables) is relevant: see §4.
- OpenEvolve (https://github.com/algorithmicsuperintelligence/openevolve) has circle-packing examples only.  FrontierCS (arXiv:2512.15699) is a benchmark.  I could not confirm that it contains a squares task **[unverified]**.

## 2. Circle-packing record finders

- **Monotonic basin hopping (MBH)**: Leary, "Global optimization on funneling landscapes", *J. Global Optim.* 18 (2000) 367–383 **[unverified pages]**.  **Addis, Locatelli, Schoen**, "Disk packing in a square: a new global optimization approach", *INFORMS J. Comput.* 20 (2008) 516–524, doi:10.1287/ijoc.1080.0263 [verified].  The method is MBH: perturb all centres within a radius, local NLP solve (SNOPT), accept only on improvement.  The key trick is a funnel conjecture: good solutions cluster, so small perturbations of the incumbent beat restarts.  They improved 32 instances for n ≤ 130, the smallest being n = 53.  **Population basin hopping** with a dissimilarity measure keeps diverse funnels.  **Grosso, Jamali, Locatelli, Schoen**, *J. Global Optim.* 47 (2010) 63–81, doi:10.1007/s10898-009-9458-3 [verified], applies MBH/PBH to circles in a circle with many Packomania records.  No official code.  Transfers directly: it is what our basin hopping already approximates.  The difference is that they solve a *constrained* max-min NLP rather than a penalty, so contacts are exact at each local solution.
- **Quasi-physical / quasi-human (Huang Wenqi school)**: Huang & Ye, "Quasi-physical global optimization method for solving the equal circle packing problem", *Sci. China Inf. Sci.* 54 (2011) 1333–1339, doi:10.1007/s11432-011-4270-3 [verified].  The energy is the sum of squared overlaps (same as ours), minimized by gradient methods.  The "quasi-human" part is a rule for jumping out: when stuck, take the circle with the largest "pain" (overlap energy) and relocate it to a vacancy.  He, Mo, Ye, Huang, *Comput. Ind. Eng.* 66 (2013) 1049 [verified] is a coarse-to-fine variant.
- **Greedy vacancy search**: Huang & Ye, *Oper. Res. Lett.* 38 (2010) 378–382, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2010.07.004 [verified].  It finds vacancies (holes) in the current configuration and greedily moves the worst circle into the best vacancy, then re-minimizes.  Several Packomania circles-in-square records came from this.  **Transfer**: the obvious analogue for us is a "remove-and-reinsert at the largest hole" move, as opposed to random local moves.
- **Iterated dynamic thresholding search (IDTS)**: Lai, Hao, Yue, Lü, Fu, *Eur. J. Oper. Res.* 299 (2022) 137–153, PDF https://leria-info.univ-angers.fr/~jinkao.hao/papers/Laietal2021EJOR.pdf [verified].  It combines a two-phase local optimizer (penalty energy, then tighten), a threshold-accepting search that accepts worse solutions within a dynamic threshold, and an explicit **container adjustment** procedure.  It improved many records for n ≤ 320 circles in a circle.  Follow-ups by the same group are perturbation-based thresholding search and solution-hashing search: Zhou, He, He, *EJOR* 327 (2025) 58–83, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2025.05.003 [verified], whose key trick is **hashing layout-graph (contact-graph) signatures to avoid revisiting the same structure**.
- **Formulation space search / reformulation descent**: Mladenović, Plastria, Urošević, *Comput. Oper. Res.* 32 (2005) 2419–2434, doi:10.1016/j.cor.2004.03.010, and LNCS 2007, doi:10.1007/978-3-540-74446-7_20 [verified].  When a local solver stalls in Cartesian coordinates, switch to polar (or mixed) coordinates for a subset of circles and re-solve.  The optimum is the same but the stationary points differ, so you escape.  **Transfer**: switch between (x, y, θ) and other coordinates, e.g. "edge-contact" coordinates (position along a neighbour's edge) or a rigid-group frame.
- **Lubachevsky–Stillinger / billiards**: Lubachevsky & Stillinger, *J. Stat. Phys.* 60 (1990) 561 [verified].  Lubachevsky & Graham's curved hexagonal packings (1997) **[unverified citation]**.  This is event-driven MD with growing particles, and it produced early circle records.  **Specht** (Packomania) has not published his method **[unverified, apart from site remarks]**.
- **Stoyan phi-functions**: Chernov, Stoyan, Romanova, "Mathematical model and efficient algorithms for object packing problem", *Comput. Geom.* 43 (2010) 535–553, doi:10.1016/j.comgeo.2009.12.003 [verified].  Bennell, Scheithauer, Stoyan, Romanova, *Ann. Oper. Res.* 179 (2010) 343–368 **[unverified pages]**.  Phi-functions give closed-form non-overlap functions for rotated rectangles and polygons.  Their method starts from a feasible point, identifies the active constraint subset, solves the NLP on that subset with IPOPT, and iterates.  It transfers exactly (rotated rectangles are a core case).

## 3. Nesting / polygon packing and physics methods

- **Egeblad, Nielsen, Odgaard**, "Fast neighborhood search for two- and three-dimensional nesting problems", *EJOR* 183 (2007) 1249–1266, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2005.11.063 [verified].  It uses guided local search on total overlap *area*.  The key trick is an **exact 1-D move**: for one polygon, it computes the overlap-area-minimizing position along a whole horizontal/vertical line in O(n log n).  It also uses GLS penalties on pairs that keep overlapping.  This applies to squares with rotation fixed per move.  An exact line search over the full container is a *global* move, unlike L-BFGS.
- **Umetani, Yagiura, Imahori, Imamichi, Nonobe, Ibaraki**, *ITOR* 16 (2009) 661–683, doi:10.1111/j.1475-3995.2009.00707.x [verified].  Guided local search for overlap minimization with no-fit-polygon (NFP) penetration depth, plus a fast exact "find minimum-overlap position along a direction" move.
- **Imamichi, Yagiura, Nagamochi**, "An iterated local search algorithm based on nonlinear programming for the irregular strip packing problem", *Discrete Optim.* 6 (2009) 345–361 [verified via author page].  The core is *separation by NLP* (penetration-depth penalty, quasi-Newton, essentially our energy).  The new element is a **swap move**: exchange two polygons and find each one's least-overlap position.  Iterated local search then shrinks the strip on success.  For congruent squares, a swap is a no-op, but the analogous "relocate one square to its global min-overlap position" is not.
- **Sparrow / jagua-rs**: Gardeyn, Vanden Berghe, Wauters, "An open-source heuristic to reboot 2D nesting research", arXiv:2509.13329 (2025), with code at https://github.com/JeroenGar/sparrow (Rust).  The collision engine is jagua-rs, *INFORMS J. Comput.* (2025), doi:10.1287/ijoc.2024.1025 [verified].  The strip-length problem is decomposed into a sequence of fixed-size *feasibility* problems.  Each one resolves collisions with GLS-weighted overlap and sampled relocations (global plus focused samples around the item), then a coordinate-descent refinement.  On success the strip shrinks, and on failure it rolls back.  It reports beating the state of the art by wide margins.  **This is the closest public, modern, state-of-the-art analogue to our fixed-side + squeeze architecture.  It is Rust, rotations are discrete in the benchmarks, and adding continuous rotation is plausible.**
- **Adaptive shrinking cell (ASC)**: Torquato & Jiao, *Nature* 460 (2009) 876, and *Phys. Rev. E* 80 (2009) 041104 [verified].  Particles and the *cell shape/size* are optimized together.  It uses MC trial moves plus a stochastic cell deformation and compression, with sequential LP for spheres (Torquato & Jiao, *PRE* 82 (2010) 061302, arXiv:1008.2747).  Key trick: the **container is a variable inside the inner loop**, not an outer continuation.
- **Divide and concur** (projection/RRR): Kallus, Elser, Gravel, "Method for dense packing discovery", *PRE* 82 (2010) 056707 [verified].  Each pair constraint is a separate copy, and alternating projections with difference-map dynamics are used.  It found new dense tetrahedra packings from random starts, and handles hard constraints without penalty ill-conditioning.
- **Event-chain MC for hard polyhedra**: Klement, Lee, Anderson, Engel, "Newtonian event-chain Monte Carlo and collision prediction with polyhedral particles", arXiv:2104.06829 (2021), implemented in HOOMD-blue (public) [verified].  Also Anderson et al., *J. Comput. Phys.* 254 (2013) 27 (GPU hard-polygon MC) [verified].  These are equilibration tools.  In compression mode (NPT, pressure → ∞) they are a principled "shake while squeezing".  Rotations are handled by separate rotation moves.

## 4. Jamming, lattice attractors, structure search, rigidity LPs

- **LP jamming test**: Donev, Torquato, Stillinger, Connelly, "A linear programming algorithm to test for jamming in hard-sphere packings", *J. Comput. Phys.* 197 (2004) 139–166, doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2003.11.022 [verified].  Linearize the contacts; an LP finds an unjamming motion (a first-order flex) or certifies that the packing is (collectively/strictly) jammed.  For a container squeeze, the LP is "maximize the shrink rate subject to linearized non-overlap".  **Directly portable to rotated squares**, since SAT contacts are piecewise-linear in (x, y) and linear in dθ to first order.  This gives a rigorous "can this configuration shrink at all?" test and a descent direction.  It is also the core step of Torquato–Jiao sequential LP.  Donev et al., "Underconstrained jammed packings of nonspherical hard particles", *PRE* 75 (2007) 051304 **[unverified pages]** shows that non-spherical particles jam with fewer contacts than isostatic, which is relevant because axis-aligned square rows are highly degenerate.
- **Integer-side lattice attractor.**  I found no paper that addresses "rows of k squares, side exactly k" as a named phenomenon.  The closest treatments are:
  - (a) Ellsworth's corner-anchored snap and 45° enumeration (§1), which embrace the lattice and search over *where* the tilted part goes;
  - (b) Chung–Graham and Göbel constructions, which are analytical gadgets;
  - (c) Zhou–He–He solution hashing on contact graphs, which avoids re-entering the same structure.

  The circle literature handles analogous hexagonal-lattice attractors with vacancy moves (GVS) and container adjustment (IDTS), not with energy changes.
- **Structure-first search.**  Berthold et al.'s formulation makes the separating axis for each pair an explicit variable, and Phi-function active-set methods solve on a chosen contact set.  Together these support a two-level scheme: (i) enumerate or mutate a combinatorial contact structure (which pairs touch, along which edge/axis, which squares are in a rigid axis-aligned block), then (ii) solve a small NLP or Newton system exactly for s.  Ellsworth's enumerator is the degenerate case where the structure is a closed-form family.

## Ranked ideas for our failure mode (jamming into k-rows; no s(110) from s(132) seeds)

1. **Gadget/structure enumeration with an exact inner solve** (Ellsworth `enum-45deg-rect-packings.cpp`, Chung–Graham, Göbel).  Parametrize the configuration as rigid axis-aligned blocks (solved exactly, never optimized) plus a small set of tilted strips/channels (k squares at angle θ), with integer parameters for counts and positions.  Enumerate the integers and optimize only the few continuous parameters (θ, offsets).  This eliminates the lattice attractor by construction.  The extension is to let the free optimizer act only on the "defect" region near the tilted strip, with blocks frozen.
2. **LP rigidity/shrink step** (Donev et al. 2004; Torquato–Jiao SLP).  At each jammed local minimum, solve "max ds subject to linearized contacts" to get a provable shrink direction or a certificate that the configuration is jammed.  Use this as the squeeze instead of continuation on the penalty.  In integer-row jams it will certify jamming immediately, which tells the outer loop to change structure rather than wait.
3. **Sparrow-style feasibility loop with GLS-weighted overlaps and sampled global relocations** (Gardeyn et al. 2025; Egeblad 2007; Umetani 2009).  Penalize persistent pair overlaps (guided local search weights) so that the optimizer cannot sit in the same shallow compromise.  Use exact or sampled *global* relocation of single squares, not only local moves.  The public Rust code is a close architectural match.
4. **Vacancy-targeted moves** (Huang–Ye GVS, quasi-human).  Find the largest hole(s) and the worst-stressed square(s), and move the square into the hole, possibly tilted.  For the k-row failure, the hole is spread thin, so a "row-removal" move fits better: delete one square from a full row, re-slide the row, reinsert the square into the resulting slack as a tilted square.
5. **Container as an inner variable** (ASC, IDTS container adjustment).  Optimize s jointly with the configuration under the penalty, with small random cell deformations allowed (rectangular container → square).  This lets slack migrate instead of freezing at integer side.  A rectangle-then-square continuation is a cheap version.
6. **Formulation switching** (Mladenović et al.).  When L-BFGS stalls, re-solve in alternative coordinates: rigid-block coordinates (move a whole row/column at once), or contact-edge coordinates for tilted squares.  This is cheap to add on top of the existing engine.
7. **Population basin hopping plus contact-graph hashing** (Grosso et al. 2010; Zhou–He–He 2025).  Keep a population that is diverse by contact-graph signature (count of axis-aligned squares, number of tilt-angle clusters), and reject children that hash to an already-explored structure.  This directly penalizes the k-row attractor.
8. **Hard-constraint polishing** (Ellsworth `refine_packing.cpp`, Phi-function active set, Berthold et al. global NLP for small subproblems).  Once a structure is found, polish to exact contacts in high precision.  Global NLP solvers are usable only on the small continuous part of a gadget.

Evidence on current practice: as of 2025–26, the record holders for n = 29–306 use simulated annealing (Schadt and derivatives, apparently on a hard or soft-constraint energy, with no public code), Ellsworth uses closed-form gadget enumeration, and recent improvements (including s(110)) come from LLM-assisted refinement.  No published squares-specific algorithm newer than Gensane–Ryckelynck (2005) was found.
