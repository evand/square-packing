Discrete geometry · computer-assisted proof

Packing Twelve Squares

Nobody knows whether twelve unit squares fit inside a square of side less than 4. The best proven barrier had stood since 2003, at 3.7889. Here is a new one at 3.9686, machine-checked from end to end — closing about 85% of the remaining gap, and an honest account of how far it does not go.

01 · The result

A new lower bound for s(12)

Theorem. Twelve unit squares cannot be packed into any square of side smaller than 15680/3951. That is, s(12) ≥ 3.968616….

3.788854  →  3.968616

Previous published bound: 2 + 4/√5 = 3.788854… (Stromquist 2003, inherited from n = 11 by monotonicity, and listed as the record for n = 11–12 in Friedman's dynamic survey DS7). The conjectured truth is s(12) = 4.

The proof is a weighted unavoidable set: a finite set of points carrying weights, such that every unit square inside the container — at every position and every angle — captures total weight at least 1. Twelve squares with disjoint interiors would then capture at least 12, so a certificate weighing less than 12 rules them out.

The certificate has 1736 points with rational coordinates of denominator 3951, in a container of side 15680/3951, and total weight 11.9738036 — under the threshold by 0.026. A 764-point certificate proves s(12) ≥ 980/247 = 3.967611, and the first bound published here, 3920/997 = 3.931795, now needs only 224 points.

A weaker certificate is far prettier, and worth stating because a human can hold it in their head. Insist that every point carry the same weight, and the best one found is:

81 points in [0, 35/9]² such that every closed unit square inside that container, at every angle, contains at least 7 of them.

Twelve squares with disjoint interiors would need 12 × 7 = 84 point-slots and only 81 points exist, so at most ⌊81/7⌋ = 11 fit — hence s(12) ≥ 35/9 = 3.888…, already past the 2003 bound. Everything after that sentence is arithmetic a computer checks exactly.

02 · Context

Where the problem stands

Let s(n) be the side of the smallest square holding n unit squares, rotations allowed. It is known exactly for surprisingly few n.

ns(n)status
103.707107…proved — Stromquist 2003
113.877084…best packing (Trump 1979); optimality open
124  (conjectured)open — bound raised here to 3.9686
134proved — Bentz 2010
14, 15, 164proved — El Moumni, Nagamochi, area

Because s(13) = 4 is proved and s(11) < 4, the largest n with s(n) < 4 is either 11 or 12 — and deciding which is the open problem. The gap for n = 12 was [3.788854, 4]; it is now [3.9686, 4].

03 · The other direction

No packing below 4 was found

The conjecture could also fall the other way — a clever tilted packing of 12 squares into something smaller than 4 would settle it immediately. It doesn't appear to exist.

An L-BFGS + basin-hopping optimiser over the 36-dimensional configuration space (x, y, θ) × 12, validated by reproducing the known optima for n = 5, 10 and 11 to five decimal places, was run at container sides 3.90 through 3.999. Every run collapsed to the same attractor: the compressed 4 × 4 grid, with residual overlap energy exactly 0.2 ε² at side 4 − ε. Nothing below 4 turned up — consistent with the 2025–26 simulated-annealing campaigns of Schadt and Ellsworth, which improved records at n = 28, 29, 39, 41, 50… but never at 12.

04 · The certificate

What the 81 points look like

The headline number was sharpened in three steps after the first certificate was found. A certificate can be scaled up linearly — points and container together — until some square's captured weight first falls below 1; that took the first LP solution from 3.92 to 3.9318. Re-optimising the weights on fixed points, with the exact verifier supplying the violated placements as cutting planes, reached 3.9486 and then hit a cliff. Growing the point set by column generation at a larger container removed the cliff and reached 3.9686, where the LP itself can no longer get under 12 — the method's ceiling, to within about 0.001.

The points were found by linear programming. Classical proofs in this area (Göbel, Stromquist, Friedman, Nagamochi, Bentz) hand-design a set of points that every unit square must contain; the LP formulation instead lets weights be fractional and optimises them, with the covering condition enforced by cutting planes generated over a rigorous cell decomposition of the placement space, and with the point locations themselves chosen by column generation over a fine candidate grid. The optimum here came out uniform — every point weight exactly 1/5 — which is why the final statement is a plain counting argument.

05 · Verification

How it is checked

A lower bound of this kind is only worth what its verification is worth, so the covering property is established three independent ways.

Lean 4 + Mathlib

The reduction step — an unavoidable set of total weight W implies at most W squares — is fully formalised, including the scaling argument that lets a closed-square certificate control a packing. 0 sorries; axioms are only propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound.

Exact integer verifier (Rust)

Checks the covering property over the entire continuum of placements. Angles are enumerated as rational rotations θ_k = 2·arctan(k/N), so all trigonometry is rational; for each bin, a unit square at any angle in the bin contains the concentric square of side σ_k = 1/(cos δ + sin δ) at angle θ_k. For each such angle the minimum over the continuum of centres is computed exactly by an arrangement sweep — no sampling. All arithmetic in i128.

Two independent re-checks

A separately written Python implementation in exact Fraction arithmetic checks every angle bin exhaustively and agrees bin by bin on every certificate. A third check — a dense float scan over 181 angles spanning the full 0°–90° range, ~500k centres each, not using the symmetry reduction — returns the same minimum for the original 788-point certificate, 1.0000023, as the exact checks do.

Resolution robustness

Verified at N = 2000 and 6000 and 12000 (830, 2487 and 4973 bins); the uniform certificates also at N = 8000. The D4 symmetry of the point set — itself checked — reduces angles to [0°, 45°].

06 · Limits

What this does not do

It does not prove s(12) = 4, and this method never will. Here is the precise reason, which is the most useful thing the exercise produced.

By LP duality, the smallest total weight any such certificate can have at container side s equals the fractional packing number ν_f(s) — the largest total mass of a measure on unit-square placements whose covering density never exceeds 1. The method proves s(12) ≥ s exactly when ν_f(s) < 12. Numerically ν_f crosses 12 at roughly s ≈ 3.95, and it jumps to 16 at s = 4 (where the grid tiles). So:

  • the ceiling of this entire family of arguments for n = 12 is about 3.95–3.96 — not 4. The bound proved here is within about 0.02 of that ceiling;
  • the remaining distance to that ceiling is computation, not mathematics: each certificate is a large cutting-plane computation that must be driven to convergence against an exact separator over a three-dimensional continuum of placements;
  • closing the remaining gap to 4 needs what Bentz used for n = 13: case analysis layered on top of the certificate — "either some square sits in this region at this angle, or a modified certificate applies". The LP gives a natural handle on that, because when the total weight W sits just above 12 the excess W − 12 bounds how much slack the whole packing has, and every square must sit in a near-binding placement. That is the obvious next step, and it is a real research project, not a longer run.
07 · A second result

s(13) = 4, without case analysis

The same machinery, pointed at the neighbouring problem, gives a genuinely new proof of a known theorem. Thirteen unit squares need a container of side exactly 4: that is Bentz's 2010 result, and his argument is a weighted point set plus a six-case analysis of where a square is allowed to sit. Here it falls out of a single object, with no cases at all.

Theorem. There are 3621 points in the square [0,4]², carrying rational weights of total 12.955972155, such that every closed unit square inside [0,4]² — at every position and every angle — captures weight at least 1.

12.955972  <  13

Thirteen squares packed below side 4 would rescale to thirteen pairwise disjoint closed unit squares in [0,4]², capturing 13 between them without double-counting a single point. They cannot: the whole set weighs less than 13. And sixteen unit squares tile [0,4]², so 4 is attained — s(13) = 4. Certificate: certificates/rung2/s13_closed_cover_4.txt.

What a “closed cover” is, and why weight under 13 is enough

A cover here is the same object as the certificate in section 01 — weighted points that no unit square can avoid — but placed in the container of side 4 itself, rather than in a slightly smaller one. The word closed is the whole of the difficulty. A point sitting exactly on the edge of a square counts as captured. That convention is not a technicality chosen for convenience: it is what every published proof in this literature uses, because they all argue about squares of side 1 + ε for every ε > 0, and the limit of that is precisely “closed square, boundary counts”.

It is also what makes the number 13 reachable. In the opposite convention — open squares, boundary does not count — the sixteen squares of the 4×4 tiling can each be shrunk a hair and become genuinely disjoint, so any cover would have to weigh at least 16 and nothing below 16 could ever be proved. In closed semantics those sixteen squares share their boundary grid points, that obstruction evaporates, and a cover of weight 12.96 is possible. It is also close to optimal: no cover of [0,4]² can weigh less than 12.2688, proved exactly in search/COVER4.md. The gap between that floor and 13 is the entire room this proof has.

“Margin zero” and “exhaustive”

The verifier of section 05 works by chopping the range of angles into bins and shrinking each square slightly so that one shrunken square stands in for a whole bin. At the container itself that method provably cannot work: at the corner placement the only point captured lies exactly on the square's boundary, so the slack is zero, and tilting by ε buys back only about ε² while any bin of width ε erodes the square by about ε. Quadratic margin against linear erosion: no resolution is fine enough.

So the cover is checked a different way. The three-dimensional space of placements — two coordinates for the centre, one for the angle — is cut adaptively into boxes with rational corners. For each box the checker must prove, exactly, that every placement in it captures weight at least 1; if it cannot, it splits the box and tries again. Exhaustive means the process finished: every box ended up certified, none was abandoned at the depth limit, and zero boxes are left uncertified. There is no sampling anywhere, and no floating-point number on the load-bearing path.

One structural fact is worth knowing, because it says why this proof looks the way it does. A theorem in search/RUNG2.md shows that if every box is certified by a fixed set of witness points — the same points captured at every placement in the box — then the cover must weigh at least 16, always. So a 12.96 cover cannot be proved that way, and most of its boxes have to be certified disjunctively instead: “at every placement here, either this set of points is inside the square, or that one is.” Both checkers below measure that the disjunctive rule carries 65% of their non-empty boxes — the same fraction, from two different disjunctions over two different subdivisions.

The two were written from the mathematical statement, not from each other: different subdivision rules, different sets of primitives, no shared line of code. They also agree pointwise, which is worth more than the box counts — asked for the exact weight captured at four specific placements, including one where a point sits exactly on the square's edge, they return the same nine-digit rationals. Twenty-three rejection tests check that the Rust checker refuses what it should: mutated certificates, two invalid covers from the project's own history, and nine malformed files, on which it must give no verdict at all rather than a wrong one.

The soundness of every primitive both checkers rest on is proved in Lean 4 + Mathlib (lean/Sqpack/ZeroMargin.lean, 36 theorems, 0 sorries, standard axioms only). What is not in Lean, and should be said plainly: the subdivision itself, the argument that the boxes exhaust the space, the bookkeeping and the file parser, in either checker. What stands behind those is that two independent programs reach zero uncertified boxes on the same file.
